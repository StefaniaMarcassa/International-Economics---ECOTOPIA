International Macroeconomics of the Euro Area

A Modern, Model-Based Approach — Europe Focus, Interactive Edition

Course Syllabus · Session 0 (toolkit) + 5 model sessions × 3h (21 teaching hours) · final exam scheduled separately · CY Cergy Paris Université

Built on the slide decks for Schmitt-Grohé, Uribe & Woodford, International Macroeconomics: A Modern Approach (Princeton, 2022), publicly posted at columbia.edu/~mu2166/UIM. The model sequence is unchanged; US examples and cases are replaced with euro-area counterparts, and each session is anchored by a short video and a live data exercise students run themselves.

## Course Description

This course follows the Uribe–Schmitt-Grohé UIM model of the open economy — a rigorous, model-based treatment built around a single intertemporal framework — and re-anchors it on the euro area. It covers, in order, the balance of payments and current-account sustainability, the intertemporal theory of the current account (endowment and production economies), the real exchange rate, international capital mobility, and monetary policy under alternative exchange-rate regimes, closing with currency pegs and balance-of-payments crises.

The distinctive European twist is that monetary union is treated as the limiting case of a hard fix: euro members share one interest rate and one currency, so the adjustment burden that a floating economy carries on its nominal exchange rate is redirected onto relative prices, wages, and employment. The euro periphery is the course's running laboratory.

The class combines 2nd- and 3rd-year undergraduates and does not assume a prior macroeconomics course. Session 0 supplies the five pieces of vocabulary the model sequence relies on: the national accounting identities, stocks versus flows, real versus nominal, discounting and present value, and exchange-rate conventions. It is taught in class and ends with a short graded check. The ungraded diagnostic at the start of Session 1 then measures whether it landed and tells the instructor which tool to revisit. Every model is built graphically first (no calculus required) and only then formalized algebraically, so the graphical route is the shared core and the algebra is the extension for stronger students.

## Interactive Design

With one toolkit session and five model sessions of three hours each, every block is anchored by two active-learning elements: a short video (primary-source clip or neutral explainer) to open the mechanism, and a live data exercise where students pull real euro-area series themselves rather than seeing a static chart. All links are collected per session below and again in the session table.

Videos are kept short (2–10 min) and neutral — primary sources (e.g. the Draghi speech) or mechanism explainers, not polemical documentaries — and are used to motivate a model, then dropped.

Data exercises use free, maintained public portals (ECB Data Portal, Eurostat, the Osnabrück Euro Crisis Monitor) so students can reproduce every figure and extend it in a problem set.

Each exercise has a 10-minute in-class 'core' version (pull one series, read it off the model) and an optional 'extension' (download the CSV, compute a ratio or spread) for the algebra track.

## Session-by-Session Plan with Media

### Session 0 — The Macro Toolkit (prerequisite session)

No chapter. Five tools the model sequence assumes, each with a worked numerical example and a live data exercise: the national accounting identities, ending at CA = S − I; stocks versus flows and the NIIP accumulation relation; real versus nominal, including the Fisher relation and a first look at the real exchange rate; discounting, present value and the two-period budget constraint, with no-Ponzi and transversality stated in words; and reading an exchange-rate quote, closing on the observation that inside the union the nominal rate is frozen at one, so the whole adjustment has to run through prices and wages. The session ends with a graded toolkit check.

### Session 1 — Global Imbalances, CA Sustainability & the Intertemporal CA

Chapters 1–3. BOP accounting, NIIP and sustainability, the two-period budget constraint and the endowment model. Framed on intra-euro imbalances, with TARGET2 balances as the visible financial-account counterpart to periphery current-account deficits. 

### Session 2 — CA in a Production Economy & Fiscal Policy

Chapters 5 and 8. Investment/saving/CA schedules and adjustment to productivity shocks; twin deficits and Ricardian equivalence, applied to the Spanish and Irish convergence booms and the Stability & Growth Pact.

### Session 3 — The Real Exchange Rate inside a Monetary Union

Chapters 9–10. PPP and its deviations, the TNT model, sudden stops. The conceptual pivot of the course: with no nominal exchange rate, 'real depreciation' means falling relative wages and prices — the periphery's 'internal devaluation'.

### Session 4 — International Capital Mobility & the Euro

Chapters 11–12. Covered and uncovered interest parity; capital controls and their effects on consumption, investment and welfare. The forward-premium puzzle is flagged as an open research question rather than developed. Framed on euro-sovereign spread compression before 2008, the 2010–12 re-fragmentation, the sovereign-bank doom loop, and the Cyprus (2013) and Greece (2015) capital controls inside the union.

### Session 5 — Currency Pegs, Crises & the Euro Capstone

Chapters 14–15. Boom-bust under a peg, the anatomy of a BoP crisis, OCA theory, and the lender-of-last-resort backstop. Case studies: EMS 1992, the euro-area sovereign-debt crisis 2010–2012, and Greece as the periphery archetype.

## Core Readings & Resources

Schmitt-Grohé, S., Uribe, M., & Woodford, M. — International Macroeconomics: A Modern Approach, Princeton University Press, 2022 (primary text; Ch.1–5, 8–15).

Uribe & Schmitt-Grohé UIM lecture slides by chapter — columbia.edu/~mu2166/UIM — projected in class and for self-study.

Obstfeld, M. & Rogoff, K. — Foundations of International Macroeconomics, MIT Press, 1996 (supplementary, formal treatment of intertemporal trade).

Data portals for the in-class exercises: ECB Data Portal, Eurostat database, Euro Crisis Monitor (Osnabrück), ECB Explainers.

Bianchi, F., Fang, Q., Melosi, L. & Rogantini Picco, A. — “Why Europe needs Eurobonds”, VoxEU/CEPR, 24 August 2026 (short policy column, assigned in Session 6; the underlying two-country DSGE model is Bianchi, Fang, Melosi & Rogantini Picco, Who is Afraid of Eurobonds?, NBER WP 35510, 2026 — reference only, not assigned).

European source packs (compiled, provided separately): the ERM/EMS crisis of 1992 (sterling & lira exit); the euro-area sovereign-debt crisis 2010–2012; and optimum-currency-area theory as the union backdrop.
