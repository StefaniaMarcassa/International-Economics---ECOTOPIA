Four real episodes

Read what happened, decide which lever moves and in which direction, set the model, then check. The settings are stylised rather than fitted: what matters is the direction and the rough size, not a match.

Spain · 1999–2007

Joining the euro

Convergence to northern European incomes was widely expected, and the rate Spain borrowed at fell close to Germany's.

Which lever moves, and which way?

Expected future income up, interest rate down. Both widen the deficit, and they did: Spain's current account deficit reached 8.9% of GDP in 2006 and passed 10% in 2007.
Royo, After Austerity: Lessons from the Spanish Experience.
Greece · 2008 → 2015

The same story in reverse

The crisis revealed that the convergence Greece had borrowed against was not coming. Expected future income was revised sharply down.

If good news widens a deficit, what does bad news do?

The deficit closed. Greece averaged 15.15% of GDP in 2007–2008 and 1.58% in 2013–2019 — a swing of about fourteen points. The model gets the direction right and most of the size, but it assumes Greece could still borrow freely. It could not, and that part is session 4.
SUERF Policy Brief, The Greek Current Account Deficit: A Persistent Problem.
European Union · 2022

The energy price spike

Gas and oil prices jumped after the invasion of Ukraine. Almost everyone expected the spike to pass rather than to last forever.

Temporary or permanent, and what follows?

Europe financed it instead of cutting spending. The EU surplus fell from €412.7bn in 2021 to €6.4bn in 2022 — essentially zero — then recovered to a record €475.8bn by 2024. Textbook temporary shock, start to finish.
Eurostat, Balance of payments statistics.
Euro area · right now

The one with no answer yet

Conflict in the Middle East has cut oil and LNG flows since February. Crude rose about 65% in two months.

Set it the same way as 2022. Is it as big?

The surplus is falling: 1.9% of GDP in Q2 2025, 1.4% in Q2 2026. That is about a fifth of the 2022 swing so far. The ECB treats it as temporary and projects a recovery to roughly 1.5% by 2028. Whether that is right is not yet known — the next release is in January.
Eurostat and ECB balance of payments, 2 October 2026; ECB Economic Bulletin 4/2026.
